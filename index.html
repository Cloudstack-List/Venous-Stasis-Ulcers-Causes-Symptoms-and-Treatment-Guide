Venous Stasis Ulcers Causes Symptoms and Treatment Guide

Venous stasis ulcers are chronic leg wounds caused by poor blood circulation in the veins. These venous stasis ulcers develop when blood pools in the lower legs due to venous insufficiency, increasing pressure that damages the skin and underlying tissue. Without proper care, they can become slow-healing or recurring wounds that impact daily comfort and mobility.

This guide explains the causes, symptoms, healing process, and treatment options based on clinical approaches used at Sheen Vein (Aesthetics and Functional Medicine) in St. Louis, MO, where care focuses on improving circulation and supporting long-term recovery.

What Are Venous Stasis Ulcers

A type of chronic wound that develops when veins in the legs fail to properly return blood back to the heart. This condition is known as venous insufficiency. When blood flow becomes inefficient, pressure builds in the lower extremities. Over time, this pressure damages the skin and underlying tissues, leading to open sores that may be slow to heal.

These venous ulcers are often linked to abnormal or weakened vein function in and around the affected area. When multiple vein abnormalities are present, they interfere with normal blood flow through the skin’s dermal layers. This reduced circulation limits oxygen and nutrient delivery to the tissue, increasing the risk of skin breakdown and delayed healing.

As pressure continues to build, the skin may become fragile, discolored, and prone to injury. Even minor trauma can lead to ulcer formation. In more advanced cases, the lack of proper circulation prevents the wound from healing naturally, causing the ulcer to persist or recur over time. This is why effective venous ulcers treatment must focus not only on the wound itself but also on restoring healthy blood flow throughout the leg.

Causes of Venous Stasis Ulcers

Venous ulcers develop due to long-term venous insufficiency, where vein valves weaken and blood flow becomes disrupted.

Primary causes include:

  • Chronic venous insufficiency
  • Weak or damaged vein valves
  • Blood pooling in the lower legs

Common risk factors:

  • Prolonged standing or sitting
  • Obesity
  • Older age
  • History of deep vein thrombosis
  • Varicose veins
  • Peripheral artery disease

A vascular care review published in clinical wound literature reports that chronic venous insufficiency is responsible for a large majority of non-healing leg ulcers, especially in older adults with circulatory conditions.

Symptoms of Venous Stasis Ulcers

Symptoms typically develop gradually and worsen over time if circulation is not corrected.

Early symptoms:

  • Swelling in the lower legs
  • Heaviness or aching sensation
  • Skin discoloration around the ankles

Advanced symptoms:

  • Open wounds that do not heal
  • Recurrent ulcers in the same area
  • Redness and inflammation
  • Fluid leakage from the wound
  • Thickened or hardened skin around the ulcer

These symptoms are often linked to ongoing venous insufficiency that continues to restrict proper blood flow.

How Venous Stasis Ulcers Are Diagnosed

Diagnosis involves identifying both the ulcer and the underlying vein dysfunction.

Clinical evaluation:

  • Physical examination of the affected area
  • Review of symptoms and medical history

Imaging tests:

  • Doppler ultrasound to assess blood flow
  • Venous duplex ultrasound to detect venous insufficiency

Identifying the underlying circulation problem is essential because treatment must address both the wound and the vein dysfunction.

How Venous Stasis Ulcers Heal

Healing depends on restoring proper venous circulation and supporting tissue repair. At treatment focuses on improving blood flow throughout the entire leg, not just the ulcer site.

Step 1: Restoring circulation

Compression therapy is used to reduce swelling and improve venous return. This helps lower pressure in the veins and supports healing.

Step 2: Wound care management

Standard care includes:

  • Cleaning and protecting the ulcer
  • Applying appropriate dressings
  • Preventing infection

Step 3: Treating venous insufficiency

Long-term healing requires addressing the root cause of poor circulation.

This may include:

  • Lifestyle changes such as regular movement
  • Weight management support
  • Reducing long periods of standing or sitting

Step 4: Advanced vein treatment options

Depending on severity, treatment may include:

  • Minimally invasive vein procedures
  • Closure of malfunctioning veins
  • Targeted vascular treatments to improve circulation

Why Venous Stasis Ulcers Are Slow to Heal

Venous ulcers take longer to heal compared to other wounds due to:

  • Ongoing poor blood circulation
  • Increased pressure in leg veins
  • Skin tissue damage from prolonged swelling
  • Reduced oxygen delivery to affected tissue

Without correcting venous insufficiency, ulcers may continue to return even after temporary healing.

Prevention Strategies

Preventing venous ulcers focuses on improving circulation and reducing vein pressure.

Helpful strategies:

  • Regular walking or leg movement
  • Avoiding long periods of sitting or standing
  • Maintaining a healthy body weight
  • Using compression therapy when recommended
  • Early evaluation of vein problems

Early intervention can significantly reduce the risk of ulcer formation.

FAQ

What causes venous stasis ulcers?

Venous stasis ulcers are caused by chronic venous insufficiency, where blood pools in the lower legs due to weak vein valves.

How do venous stasis ulcers heal?

Healing requires restoring blood flow, reducing swelling through compression therapy, and treating underlying vein disease.

Can venous stasis ulcers go away on their own?

They rarely heal without medical treatment because the underlying circulation problem must be corrected.

What is the best treatment for venous stasis ulcers?

The most effective approach combines compression therapy, wound care, and treatment of venous insufficiency.

Are venous stasis ulcers serious?

Yes, they can become chronic and worsen without proper treatment, leading to infection or long-term tissue damage.
